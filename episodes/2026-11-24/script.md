# Nvidia Reinstates Jensen Huang, Anthropic's Centimillionaires | OWITH Daily

Good morning from OWITH.ai, the briefing that gives you only what's important to hear in AI and tech. It's Tuesday, November 24.

Today, Jensen Huang is back in charge at Nvidia, and Anthropic's listing turns hundreds of its employees into centimillionaires.

An update on the story we covered last Wednesday. On Monday, Nvidia's board voted unanimously to reinstate Jensen Huang as chief executive and director, less than two weeks after he resigned. The board now says it took input from senior levels of the United States government and is confident his return poses no national security risk. President Trump said he convinced the board. Fortune reports the shares rose about thirty percent at the open. The investigations continue. The SEC and the California attorney general are examining Nvidia, President Trump says the FBI is looking at Huang's ties to a woman suspected of links to Chinese intelligence, and Nvidia has created a board committee for national security and export control. Investors got their leader back, along with every open question.

Next, the human side of Anthropic's listing. Fortune reports that five hundred ninety eight employees who are not founders now hold at least one hundred million dollars each, according to the investment firm Hill, as cited by Reuters. That gives Anthropic more centimillionaires than London, Paris or Los Angeles, using data from Henley and Partners. The average employee who is not a founder holds over forty four million dollars. Oxfam America warns the boom could deepen economic divides. Dario Amodei has made the same warning himself, and he and his six cofounders have pledged to give eighty percent of their equity to philanthropy. Expect inequality to move to the center of the AI policy debate.

Oracle shows the other side of the AI trade. Its shares fell about seven and a half percent at the end of last week to roughly one hundred two dollars, the lowest level since January twenty twenty four, and they are down forty seven percent over the past year. Investors are worried about heavy AI infrastructure spending, rising debt and negative free cash flow. Berkeley economist Bradford DeLong told Fortune the slide reflects second thoughts about Oracle's cloud deal with OpenAI. The drop cost Larry Ellison ten point nine billion dollars and pushed him out of the world's top ten richest people. For boards, the lesson is that AI capacity funded with debt gets judged quickly.

In Tokyo, Japan Exchange Group chief executive Hiromi Yamaji told Fortune that Japan should hold up better than its neighbors as the AI trade cools. AI and semiconductor stocks make up only eleven point three percent of the TOPIX index, she said, against sixty percent in Korea and forty seven percent in Taiwan. Last week the TOPIX fell seven percent while Taiwan's main index fell thirteen. Foreign buying of Japanese stocks reached about sixty eight billion dollars in the first ten months of this year, double last year's total. For global investors, Japan is becoming a way to stay in Asia with less exposure to chips.

The AI boom is also squeezing phone makers. Fortune reports that smartphone companies are reluctant to match the prices that AI labs and data centers pay for processors, and that has led to shortages. The research firm Omdia expects smartphone shipments to fall almost twelve percent this year, the largest contraction ever, followed by another six percent drop in twenty twenty seven. Oppo, the world's fourth largest maker, sees an opening in Europe. Its European chief, Elvis Zhou, wants ten percent of the premium market and argues that AI search tools help challenger brands by focusing buyers on features.

Finally, the job market. LinkedIn chief executive Daniel Shapero told Fortune the market is frozen and harder for people early in their careers. He also says young candidates have an edge, because employers assume they are more attuned to AI and want them to help transform how the company works. His advice is to network directly, since the fastest path to a job runs through someone already at the company. Leaders hiring for AI fluency should test for it, because age alone proves little.

The thread today is that AI money is concentrating fast, in a single chief executive, in one company's staff, and in the chips everyone else now competes for. That's it for today. Thanks for listening to OWITH.ai. See you tomorrow.
