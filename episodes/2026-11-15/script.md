# OpenAI Disbands Robotics Team, FAA Ground Stop Explained | OWITH Daily

Good morning from OWITH.ai, the briefing that gives you only what's important to hear in AI and tech. It's Sunday, November 15.

Today, OpenAI folds its robotics team into its core research, and a routine wiring job turns out to be behind Friday's nationwide flight ground stop.

Fortune reports exclusively that OpenAI has disbanded its standalone robotics team. Robotics researchers now sit in the main research group under Chief Research Officer Mark Chen, while applied robotics and hardware move to the organization run by President Greg Brockman. OpenAI says robotics remains core to its long term vision, and that its general model, GPT-6 Astra, can drive robots directly. In one Nvidia study, an Astra powered agent reached a success rate of nearly seventy five percent on a simulated manipulation test, against fifty one percent for the best specialized models. Why it matters: the frontier labs are betting that general intelligence will absorb robotics. Specialists counter that real factory data still gives them an edge.

On Friday the FAA ordered a nationwide ground stop lasting several hours, and airlines delayed or canceled thousands of flights. The White House situation room and the FBI were activated over fears of a foreign cyberattack. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy now says no foreign actor was involved. A contractor for L3Harris, switching telecom lines from copper to fiber at a remote FAA site, flooded routers with more than a thousand connection attempts every few seconds, disrupting radar feeds. Why it matters: the modernization of critical infrastructure is itself a source of risk, and L3Harris says the conversion is more than seventy percent done.

The same week brought a Microsoft outage. On Thursday, email, Outlook and Teams failed for many customers, and the damage spread into public life. Fortune reports that police forces in Los Angeles and Britain struggled to receive online crime reports, New York City suspended Friday evictions, and the NHS had trouble reaching appointments and patient data. Microsoft blamed a flaw from an earlier network capacity upgrade and ruled out a cyberattack. Why it matters: with more than four hundred fifty million paid commercial seats, a single vendor's bad change can now stall courts and hospitals across several continents.

Local voters have sent the data center industry a clear signal. In an analysis republished by Fortune, political scientist Barbara Trish counts forty one data center measures on midterm ballots across ten states, using Ballotpedia data. All but one restricted development, by an average margin of forty nine points. The lone exception was Harwood, North Dakota, where a project was already underway. Why it matters: with more than six hundred data centers proposed, local permission is becoming as scarce as power, and candidates in both parties are taking note.

Power is the business plan at Keel Infrastructure, the former Bitcoin miner Bitfarms. Fortune profiles chief executive Ben Gagnon, who is turning the company into a developer of powered sites for AI tenants, with two point two gigawatts approved or close to approval. The catch: Keel had guided to leases at three priority projects by year end, then said on its earnings call that no deals would be signed this year. The stock fell about twenty percent. Why it matters: grid access is valuable, but the market rewards signed tenants.

Now an update on the Nvidia story we covered on Friday. The Senate Banking Committee, chaired by Tim Scott, has asked former chief executive Jensen Huang and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick to testify in December, citing significant national security concerns and its oversight of chip export controls. The committee says it is prepared to use subpoenas if either declines. Ranking member Elizabeth Warren wants answers on why export loopholes remain open. Why it matters: the scandal has moved from Nvidia's boardroom to export policy, where the stakes reach every American chipmaker.

Finally, a reality check for agentic commerce. A Bank of America survey reported by Fortune finds that seventy two percent of Americans don't plan to use AI for holiday shopping, up from forty nine percent last year. Many say it removes the personal side of giving a gift. Those who do plan to use it mostly want to compare prices. Why it matters: Google, Meta and OpenAI are all building shopping agents, and consumers are signaling they want help with the chores of buying, while keeping the choice for themselves.

The thread today is trust: in networks, in neighbors, and in the machines we hand our decisions to. That's it for today. Thanks for listening to OWITH.ai. See you tomorrow.
