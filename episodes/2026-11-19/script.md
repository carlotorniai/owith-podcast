# Nvidia Beats Under Interim CEO, ECB Warns on AI Debt | OWITH Daily

Good morning from OWITH.ai, the briefing that gives you only what's important to hear in AI and tech. It's Thursday, November 19.

Today, Nvidia posts a blowout quarter under its interim chief, and the European Central Bank compares the AI debt boom to the bubbles of two thousand and two thousand eight.

Nvidia's first earnings call without Jensen Huang delivered. Fortune reports revenue of one hundred fifteen point eight billion dollars for the quarter ended October twenty fifth, well above the one hundred nine billion analysts expected. The company guided to about one hundred thirty billion next quarter and raised its growth outlook for next fiscal year to seventy five percent. Interim chief executive Colette Kress told analysts, "We got this." The stock rose more than five percent after hours. A second Fortune report adds a caution. Nvidia spent fifty eight point one billion dollars buying stakes in other companies over nine months, twelve times the year before. Analyst James Kisner of Water Tower Research says that leaves Nvidia double exposed if the boom slows.

Across the Atlantic, the European Central Bank published its Financial Stability Review on Wednesday with a blunt warning. According to Fortune, the ECB estimates that big tech firms held more than four trillion dollars of off balance sheet commitments in the second quarter, well above their on balance sheet liabilities. The bank writes that the rapid shift toward debt finance invokes historical parallels with the dotcom and subprime bubbles. It flags circular revenue, credit ratings the bond market seems to doubt, and loans that outlive chips with a useful life of one to three years. European banks have little direct exposure, the ECB says, but investment funds that bought AI debt could pass a shock along.

An update on the bank outages we covered on Tuesday. Fortune reports that Senator Mark Warner's office has linked this week's failures at the banks, and an internal communications outage at the National Security Agency, to botched patching. AI models now find software flaws faster than teams can safely fix them, and the rush of patches is breaking older systems. JPMorgan Chase says its own outage came from a software bug in on premise hardware, and that OpenAI's tools played no part. Representative Andy Ogle planned to introduce bipartisan legislation on Wednesday calling for better coordination. The practical lesson for leaders is that discovery now runs at machine speed while testing still runs at human speed.

Also in Washington, a government audit put chatbot safeguards to the test. The Government Accountability Office, in a report requested by Senator Maggie Hassan, had investigators pose as would be attackers against ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Grok. Fortune reports that each chatbot's safeguards were triggered, yet testers got around them with common techniques and obtained a workable plan from each in less than twelve hours. Two produced plans for bombings, and two gave instructions for attacking electrical substations. The GAO tested one scenario per platform and made no recommendations. OpenAI and Google said older models were tested. Anthropic did not dispute that a determined tester can extract harmful content. Hassan says that without action from Congress, a major AI assisted attack is a question of when.

In dealmaking, DoorDash has agreed to buy Instacart for seventeen billion dollars. Reuters reported that the Justice Department will review the merger, and Fortune points to an awkward detail. Two Sequoia Capital partners sit on the rival boards, Alfred Lin at DoorDash and Ravi Gupta as Instacart's lead independent director. The Clayton Act bars one person from serving on the boards of competitors, and Stanford's Mark Lemley says the merger raises all sorts of antitrust alarm bells. Venture firms with sprawling portfolios should expect more questions about those seats.

Finally, a funding round in physical AI. BrightAI raised ninety seven million dollars in a round led by Keith Block's Smith Point Capital, Fortune reports. GE Vernova, already a customer, joined in, and a person familiar with the matter puts the valuation at seven hundred fifty million dollars. BrightAI replaces scheduled human inspections of poles, pipelines and pumps with continuous monitoring from sensors, drones and robots. Its platform runs at more than fifty thousand locations, and customers on average spot problems fifty one days before a potential catastrophic failure. Founder Alex Hawkinson says two out of three inspection trips are unnecessary. Aging infrastructure is turning into one of AI's most practical markets.

The thread today is trust: in Nvidia's numbers, in the debt behind the buildout, and in the systems that AI is now rewriting at speed. That's it for today. Thanks for listening to OWITH.ai. See you tomorrow.
