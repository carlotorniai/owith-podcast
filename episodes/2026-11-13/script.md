# Jensen Huang Exits Nvidia, Venture Cracks Before Anthropic IPO | OWITH Daily

Good morning from OWITH.ai, the briefing that gives you only what's important to hear in AI and tech. It's Friday, November 13.

Today we start with Jensen Huang's exit from Nvidia, then look at what PitchBook data says about the health of venture capital.

An update on the story we covered on Wednesday. Jensen Huang has resigned as chief executive of Nvidia, ending a thirty three year run at the company he cofounded, Fortune reports. The board named chief financial officer Colette Kress as interim chief executive, and appointed longtime director Harvey Jones as its first ever chair. The exit follows Huang's admission of an affair with a woman American officials consider a suspected Chinese spy, and a second report alleging a relationship with a senior Nvidia executive. Shares fell more than six percent, and a law firm has filed a class action. Why it matters: Nvidia has no chief operating officer and no clear successor, and its biggest customers now face open questions about product direction at their most important supplier.

The timing is awkward for the startup world. Fortune's Term Sheet newsletter reports that the cracks in venture are beginning to show, just as Anthropic prepares to go public as soon as next week. PitchBook data shows that reaching a five billion dollar valuation now takes eighty percent less time but seventy four percent more capital. PitchBook analyst Emily Zheng says higher valuations raise the bar for profitable exits, and points to heavy AI related debt. Outside a few mega listings, exit values remain constrained, and the sale of Airtable for one point two eight billion dollars, against an eleven point seven billion peak, shows the discounts on offer. A strong Anthropic debut would validate private AI prices. A weak one would invite hard questions.

In enterprise software, Databricks has launched agents that never switch off, according to Fortune. An updated version of its Genie assistant runs in the background, shadowing an office worker through the day, offering suggestions and pulling up information as work happens. A separate feature called Ontology maps an organization's data, including reporting lines and departmental projects, so staff can find answers and run analyses quickly. Chief executive Ali Ghodsi says he uses it every day. The launch lands one day after Anthropic released its own office tools. Databricks, valued at one hundred ninety billion dollars in August, is betting that whoever owns company data will own the workplace agent.

Europe's defense technology scene keeps drawing money. Fortune reports that SE3, a Munich startup, has raised thirty million dollars in a Series A round led by Accel, less than five months after its seed round. SE3 builds software that fuses video and sensor feeds from many drones and ground vehicles into a single three dimensional battlefield map. Soldiers can query it in plain language, and drones can navigate when GPS is jammed. The company has contracts with Germany's Bundeswehr and technology already deployed in Ukraine. It plans to triple its staff to about one hundred twenty. Investors are backing military AI that stays independent of any single hardware maker.

On trade, American importers moved a step closer to automatic tariff refunds, Fortune reports. In February the Supreme Court ruled that tariffs imposed under emergency powers exceeded the president's authority, putting about one hundred sixty six billion dollars in play. More than one hundred fifteen billion had been paid back by August. On Tuesday, lawyers for a certified class asked the Court of International Trade to order refunds for more than three hundred thirty thousand importers, whether or not they sued. The administration argues only plaintiffs should be repaid. For hardware makers and retailers that paid those duties, this is real cash, and the Justice Department must respond by December ninth.

Finally, a warning for finance chiefs. At a Fortune dinner for CFOs in New York, Boston University lecturer Mark Williams, a former Federal Reserve bank examiner, told executives to assume the cost of capital is going up. He expects AI budgets to face tougher scrutiny, while warning that companies doing nothing risk losing their edge. His advice is to set explicit measures of success for each AI project, tie spending to financial results, and favor shorter term financing. Highly rated tech giants can keep spending. More leveraged AI borrowers may need to rethink their plans.

The thread today is concentration risk, whether it sits in a single chief executive, a single IPO or a single cost of money.
