# Anthropic's Record IPO Debut, Fed Signals AI Rate Hikes | OWITH Daily

Good morning from OWITH.ai, the briefing that gives you only what's important to hear in AI and tech. It's Friday, November 20.

Today, Anthropic makes a record breaking stock market debut, and the Federal Reserve signals that the AI building boom is now an inflation problem.

Anthropic is a public company. Fortune reports the shares priced at eighty three dollars, above the marketed range, then opened at one hundred eleven dollars and fifty cents, a jump of thirty four percent that briefly valued the company near two trillion dollars. By midday the gain had narrowed to fourteen percent as the wider market fell. The offering raised seventy nine billion dollars, more than SpaceX raised in June. A second Fortune report shows who benefits most. Amazon will hold fourteen point six percent and Google thirteen point four percent, all in shares with no vote, while the seven cofounders keep control through a special class. Investors now get quarterly numbers on the first pure frontier model maker, and on a revenue base that hit thirty three billion dollars in nine months.

The bill for that boom is showing up at the central bank. Minutes of the Fed's October meeting, reported by Fortune, say almost all officials think more rate hikes are likely appropriate. Participants said demand for AI buildout products is straining supply and lifting prices of tech consumer goods, and that the pressure is spreading beyond data center construction. Traders put about ninety six percent odds on a quarter point hike in December. Apollo's Torsten Slok estimates AI investment drove roughly sixty five percent of US growth over the past three quarters. Higher rates raise the cost of every data center financed with debt.

Amazon wants software buying to run on autopilot. Fortune reports in an exclusive that AWS Marketplace is rolling out AI agents that can research, vet and purchase software for companies. Buyers give an agent a budget and decide which purchases still need a human sign off. Matt Yanchyshyn, who runs the marketplace, says a company could let an agent pick a sign in vendor and start a free trial on its own. AWS is working with Drata and Vanta so agents can check a vendor's security reports inside the marketplace. Procurement rules written for people now need a version for machines.

Robotaxis have hit a speed bump. Fortune reports that Waymo has suspended driverless freeway rides in every market, including Phoenix, Miami and Austin. The move follows a California DMV order calling its freeway operations an imminent hazard to public safety, after three Waymo vehicles drove into an active construction closure on Route 101 near Burlingame. It is the second freeway pause this year. Waymo says nobody was hurt and that its cars cause eighty two percent fewer injury crashes than human drivers. For anyone planning on autonomous logistics, regulators are now judging edge cases, and averages carry less weight.

Startup money is getting bigger without spreading wider. Fortune's Term Sheet, citing Crunchbase data, reports that US startups raised forty two billion dollars in Series A rounds through October, up sixty percent from a year earlier. The number of rounds has barely moved, holding around twelve hundred a year. Roseanne Wincek of Renegade Partners says the bar has risen. About one million dollars in annual recurring revenue once earned a Series A. Today it often earns a seed round. Expensive talent and compute mean each dollar goes less far, and capital is concentrating in fewer founders.

Finally, a familiar name is betting on physical AI in fashion. Fortune reports that Tony Fadell, a codesigner of the iPod and iPhone, has invested roughly ten million dollars in Smartex and is joining as chairman. Its new camera system attaches to existing textile machines and checks fabric quality continuously. Chief executive Max Easton says H&M, an investor and launch customer, over orders about five percent of its fabric to cover defects, and estimates Smartex could save it one hundred million dollars a year. That figure is the company's own. If it holds, quality control becomes a lever on margins.

The thread today is the cost of capital. Markets are paying record prices for AI, and the Fed is starting to charge for the boom.
