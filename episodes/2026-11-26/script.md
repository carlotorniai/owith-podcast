# Huawei Convicted in U.S. Trial, Meta and Amazon Talk Muse Deal | OWITH Daily

Good morning from OWITH.ai, the briefing that gives you only what's important to hear in AI and tech. It's Thursday, November 26.

Today, a New York jury convicts Huawei on ten counts, and Meta and Amazon sit down to negotiate the future of AI shopping agents.

We start in a federal courtroom in New York. Fortune reports that a jury on Tuesday convicted Huawei on ten of twelve counts, including bank fraud, wire fraud and conspiracy to steal trade secrets. The company was acquitted of racketeering conspiracy and conspiracy to obstruct justice. The case dates to 2018 and centers on claims that Huawei misled banks about its business in Iran. Huawei says it expects to appeal, and sentencing is set for September 2027. Dexter Tiff Roberts of the Atlantic Council expects the verdict to do little to slow the company, and thinks Beijing may lean harder on Huawei as a national champion. Its Ascend chips are China's main answer to Nvidia under export controls, and Huawei said in September it lacks the capacity to meet demand at home.

Next, a negotiation that could set the rules for AI commerce. Fortune reports, in an exclusive, that Meta and Amazon have met several times to discuss connecting Meta's Muse agent to Amazon's store. Amazon blocked Muse in September, citing its user agreements. The sticking points are search results and advertising. Amazon's ad business brings in sixty eight point six billion dollars a year, and an agent that shops on a customer's behalf could in theory bypass those ads. Andy Jassy has said outside agents would need to offer a fair exchange of value. Whatever terms emerge could become a template for every retailer facing AI agents.

Over at OpenAI, chief scientist Jakub Pachocki has proposed a way for labs to agree on a speed limit. In a blog post reported by Fortune's Jeremy Kahn, he suggests that every frontier lab publicly report its base model perplexity, a measure of how well a model predicts text. Labs would then agree on a threshold they would not cross without agreed safety measures. Microsoft AI chief Mustafa Suleyman backed the idea and published the figure for Microsoft's MAI two base model. Oxford's Robert Trager warned that competition would simply move to post training. For executives, this is the first concrete industry proposal to measure frontier progress in a common unit.

An update on the story we covered last Friday. Anthropic shares closed at eighty one dollars and ninety two cents on Tuesday, below their eighty three dollar IPO price for the first time. The stock fell nearly seven percent that day as the Nasdaq slid. Fortune reports that Wall Street is split. Needham's Scott Berg calls the dip a generational opportunity, with a one hundred fifty dollar target, and Wedbush and Canaccord Genuity also rate it a buy. Ken Mahoney of Mahoney Asset Management advises waiting for an earnings report or two. The debate will shape how easily other AI labs can follow Anthropic onto public markets.

Europe is asking whether it can win in quantum computing after falling behind in AI. Fortune reports that European nations committed fifteen point nine billion dollars to quantum technology in the five years to 2025. Private money tells a different story. Global private investment hit a record twelve point six billion dollars last year, and the European Commission estimates European firms attract about five percent of it. Europe does hold real strengths: it is home to about a third of the world's quantum companies, and Pasqal and IQM listed on Nasdaq this year. Pasqal's CEO Wasiq Bokhari says Europe has all the ingredients and needs a unified direction. For leaders, quantum is shaping up as the next test of technology sovereignty.

Finally, a look at AI inside retail operations as Black Friday arrives. Fortune reports that Gap has used Devin, the software engineering agent from Cognition, for about six months. Devin hunts for website problems, traces their causes and writes tested fixes, which human engineers then review and publish. Chief technology officer Sven Gerjets says it speeds up fixes for glitches that can grow into multi million dollar problems, and Gap processes more than fifteen hundred orders a minute at peak. Lowe's, meanwhile, deliberately shuts down its own systems to rehearse recovery. Agents are moving from demos into the systems that carry real revenue.

The thread today is control: over trade secrets, over shopping customers, over the pace of AI itself, and over who leads the next wave of computing.
