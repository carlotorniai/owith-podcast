# SpaceX's $74B AI Data Center Bet, Grid Falls Behind | OWITH Daily

Good morning from OWITH.ai, the briefing that gives you only what's important to hear in AI and tech. It's Monday, November 23.

Today, SpaceX's seventy four billion dollar bet on AI data centers runs into hard questions about power, and a new analysis shows how far the grid is falling behind.

Fortune reports that SpaceX plans to spend more than seventy four billion dollars on two AI supercomputing campuses. About fifty billion would go to southeastern Arizona, and more than twenty four billion to Starbase, Texas, where a state permit issued on November sixteenth allows thirty five gas turbines producing seven hundred fifty megawatts. The Arizona site would reach six thousand megawatts at full build out, nearly two thirds of the record peak demand of Arizona Public Service, the state's largest utility. SpaceX promises neighbors lower bills and taxes. Local officials want proof. Arizona's attorney general, Kris Mayes, has asked Cochise County to delay its December ninth vote until regulators review the project, and in Texas, Cameron County commissioners voted three to two against pursuing a ten year tax break.

The wider picture comes from an analysis by insurance broker Howden, also reported by Fortune. Since two thousand, power demand from data centers has grown about nineteen percent a year, while American grid capacity has grown about two percent. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory projects data centers could use up to twelve percent of the country's electricity by twenty twenty eight. Kathryn Burke of Marsh expects half or more of data center projects to slip past their planned timelines. The bill is the political problem. Cornell researchers found that across four grids, data centers paid only thirty eight percent of the infrastructure costs tied to their growth. Delaware now requires large energy users to secure all of their own power within ten years of opening.

In security news, two fundraising platforms used by charities were hit just before Giving Tuesday, Fortune reports. DonorPerfect, which serves eleven thousand customers, took its platform offline on November tenth after confirming unauthorized activity, and reportedly said it has a lot of evidence that AI bots were involved. Separately, GoFundMe said intruders accessed GoFundMe Pro, its platform for nonprofits, exposing donor names, addresses, emails, phone numbers and dates of birth, plus Salesforce credentials for some organizations. The timing hurts. Nonprofits collected thirty seven percent of last year's online revenue in December, and GoFundMe says it has strong reason to believe attackers are targeting other platforms too.

On the business of putting AI to work, Tribe AI says it has reached a one hundred million dollar annual revenue run rate, up three point eight times in a year. Fortune profiles the firm, founded in twenty nineteen by former Google Capital staffer Jaclyn Rice Nelson. Tribe helps companies move AI out of pilots and into production. Clients include Hyatt, MetLife and Koch, where Tribe built an AI coaching tool based on Charles Koch's management philosophy in five months. The company has about two hundred fifty staff and reportedly raised less than ten million dollars in venture funding while staying profitable. For buyers, the lesson is that integration, the unglamorous part, is where enterprise budgets are now flowing.

Fortune also reports that Remote, the HR platform valued at three billion dollars, now requires its roughly two thousand employees to take a free five hour AI course on company time. Chief executive Job van der Voort says anyone who skips it will miss a meets expectations rating in their performance review. In his words, AI will run the world, so it's about getting with the times. This moves AI fluency from encouragement to a formal job requirement. Van der Voort also argues that employers should take responsibility for training the people they hire, which puts the cost of reskilling on the company.

Finally, a survey from talent firm Seramount, covered by Fortune, finds that only eighteen percent of Gen Z office workers want to build their AI skills, compared with forty six percent of Gen Xers and forty three percent of baby boomers. Just twenty seven percent of Gen Z feel comfortable telling colleagues they used AI for a task. Wharton professor Jessica Reif says AI use feels especially risky for younger workers, and her research found that employees described as getting AI help were rated less competent and lazier. Seramount warns that if Gen Z falls behind, companies weaken their future leadership pipeline.

The thread today is that the costs of AI are landing on people outside the labs, from ratepayers and charities to employees now asked to prove their skills. That's it for today. Thanks for listening to OWITH.ai. See you tomorrow.
