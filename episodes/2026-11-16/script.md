# Bessent Turns Up Heat on Nvidia, AI Labs Hire Former Spies | OWITH Daily

Good morning from OWITH.ai, the briefing that gives you only what's important to hear in AI and tech. It's Monday, November 16.

Today, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent turns up the pressure on Nvidia, and the AI labs go on a hiring spree for former spies.

An update on the Nvidia story we covered on Friday. Fortune reports that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, speaking on Fox News on Sunday, said the key question is whether Nvidia had a culture of compliance around American export restrictions. If Nvidia were a bank that had failed to comply, he said, the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency would be inside and many executives would be removed. He urged the board to look outside the company for its next chief executive. On Friday, Bessent told CNBC that Treasury had for months backed blacklisting Megaspeed, a Singapore cloud company reported to have shipped at least seven and a half billion dollars in Nvidia chips to China. Why it matters: Washington now treats Nvidia's governance as a national security question.

The scandal has also put a price on corporate counterintelligence. Fortune, citing Revelio Labs data compiled by Reuters, reports that Anthropic has made thirty three counterintelligence hires this year, up from fourteen in twenty twenty five. OpenAI has added twenty two, up from nine, and Nvidia sixteen. Salaries mostly run from two hundred thousand to five hundred thousand dollars. About a month ago, the Justice Department arrested an OpenAI engineer for planting a listening device in a company conference room. Former FBI operative Eric O'Neill says recruitment often begins innocently, at conferences, over drinks or on dating apps. Why it matters: model weights and chip designs are now strategic assets, and protecting them means guarding people as well as networks.

Fortune also reports that humanoid robots are arriving slower than promised. Tesla says it has started producing its Optimus robot on a former car line in Fremont. Elon Musk, though, called the early ramp shallow and somewhat bumpy and expects outside deliveries around the end of twenty twenty seven. Agility Robotics, maker of the Digit robot that Amazon has tested since twenty twenty three, began trading on Nasdaq this month after a SPAC merger. It reports more than three hundred million dollars in committed multi year orders from Amazon, GXO, Schaeffler and Toyota's Canadian arm. Why it matters: humanoids are moving from demos to order books, yet timelines keep slipping, and Bloomberg data shows Chinese makers shipped over ninety seven percent of humanoids in the first half of the year.

In energy, Fortune profiles Nextpower, the solar tracker maker formerly known as Nextracker. It controls about fifty five percent of the American tracker market and about thirty percent globally. Chief executive Dan Shugar says the project backlog has roughly tripled since the company went public four years ago, to more than six billion dollars. After thirteen acquisitions in five years, including the battery storage business of Prevalon Energy, Shugar is pitching the company to the industries building power hungry AI data centers. Why it matters: about eighty percent of new American solar projects now include battery storage, which gives suppliers like Nextpower a route into the data center buildout even as federal solar and wind tax credits fade.

A quieter AI business is growing up. Fortune reports that Otter.ai, the meeting notetaker, now earns one hundred million dollars a year and has more than forty million users. The company raised three hundred twenty million dollars last month and says it is being integrated with OpenAI, Claude and Google. Chief executive Sam Liang, who cofounded it in twenty sixteen, credits frugality, saying every dollar should earn its way into the budget. Why it matters: as the big model makers push into productivity tools, focused application companies with real revenue show there is still room to build on top of the platforms.

Finally, a check on AI in the classroom. Alpha School charges up to seventy five thousand dollars a year for a two hour, AI tutored academic day and promises students learn twice as much as their peers. Internal data, first reported by the New York Times and reviewed by Fortune, shows only forty two percent of students met that goal in both math and reading. About half of the K through eight students at its private campuses missed the math target. Houston's school district, piloting the software at two schools, says students spent more time on the apps than planned. Why it matters: AI tutoring is selling to parents and public districts faster than evidence of its results is arriving.

The thread today is trust: in the people inside AI companies, in the timelines they promise, and in the results their products claim.
