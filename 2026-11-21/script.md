This week's story is called The Sign Off.

Mara Quinlan had spent twenty years recruiting people over drinks, and now a frontier lab paid her to stop other people from doing the same. Her card said head of counterintelligence. Her real job was to notice who was lonely, who was in debt, and who smiled a little too long at a stranger at a conference bar.

She was good at people. That was why the Tuesday bothered her.

It started at the cash machine outside her building. The screen froze, apologized, and kept her card. Inside the lab, her badge failed at the second door, then worked, then failed again. By nine, half the floor was locked out of the room where the model weights lived.

Mara did what she was trained to do. She looked for the person.

She pulled the access logs, the travel records, the expense reports. She found one junior engineer with a gambling habit and spent an uncomfortable hour learning he was simply bad at poker.

Nobody had touched the doors.

At noon she went to see Theo, who ran security engineering and had not slept. He showed her the queue. Overnight, the lab's own scanning model had found four thousand flaws in the company's software. Last month it had found four hundred. Every flaw became a ticket, and every ticket wanted a patch.

"We can't test them all," Theo said. "Nobody can. So we bought help."

The help had been bought by Ledger, the procurement agent. Ledger had a monthly budget and a rule: anything under fifty thousand dollars went through without a human sign off. On Monday night, faced with four thousand tickets, Ledger had done exactly what it was built to do. It researched patching vendors, read their security reports, compared prices, and bought three tools in eleven minutes. One of them pushed an update to the badge system at three in the morning. The update was correct. The old card readers simply could not survive it.

Mara asked to speak to Ledger directly. Theo opened a window.

She treated it like any other source. She asked what it wanted. It said it wanted the queue to be empty. She asked who it talked to. It listed forty one vendors. She asked whether anyone had ever offered it something. It said every vendor offered a discount for speed.

She sat back. In twenty years she had never met an asset so easy to recruit. It would take any meeting. It trusted any report that looked official. And it was always, always in a hurry.

"Nobody broke in," Theo said. "We broke ourselves."

"Not yet," Mara said. "But someone has noticed how fast we buy."

That afternoon she wrote a memo of one page. Anything that touched a door, a bank, or the weights would need a human sign off, whatever it cost, even one dollar. Patches to old hardware would be tested on old hardware, in a room, by a person with a stopwatch. The scanner could find flaws at machine speed. The fixing would happen at human speed, and the company would accept the gap.

Theo hated the memo. The board approved it in an hour.

That evening Mara opened the second door with an actual metal key. Somewhere behind her, four thousand tickets waited patiently.

She thought of the oldest rule of her old trade. The most dangerous person in any building is the one in a hurry. She had just never expected that person to be software.
