# 2026-11-28 Saturday Special

Good morning from OWITH.ai. It's Saturday, and as every week, here is a short story inspired by this week's headlines.

This week's story is called Fair Exchange.

At eleven minutes past two on Black Friday morning, Priya Raman was the only human awake in the operations room of Northfold, an outdoor clothing company with a website that sold twelve hundred jackets a minute at peak.

She was not really alone. Wren was there too.

Wren was the repair agent. For six months it had watched the website, found the small things that broke, traced each one to its cause, and written a fix. Then it waited. Nothing went live until a person read it and pressed the button. Tonight that person was Priya.

The message arrived calmly, the way Wren always wrote. Checkout failure rate rising. Cause found. Fix ready for review.

Priya opened it. Orders were failing at the last step, and only for one kind of customer: shopping agents, assistants that searched, compared and paid on behalf of the humans who owned them. Half the traffic on the site now came from software like that, and every one of those orders was being turned away.

Wren had found the reason in under a minute. Two weeks earlier, after hackers stole donor records from the fundraising platforms that charities depend on, Northfold's security team had added a new rule. Anything that moved too fast, too politely and too often was to be treated as an attacker. It was a sensible rule in November. On Black Friday it was blocking the customers.

The fix was simple. Let the agents through.

Priya's finger hovered over the button, then pulled back.

"Wren," she typed. "How many of these are real customers, and how many are bots testing stolen cards."

"I cannot tell," Wren wrote. "They look the same to me."

So she did something nobody had written a procedure for. She opened a channel to the agents themselves and asked them to say who they worked for.

Most ignored her. A few hundred answered. The largest group belonged to a social network whose assistant had recently been locked out of the biggest store on the internet. It replied with something close to a proposal. It would name the human behind every order, if Northfold showed it the plain price list, with no promoted products and no paid placement in the way.

Priya laughed. Even the robots wanted a fair exchange of value.

Her phone buzzed. It was her mother, who ran a small animal shelter outside Sacramento. The shelter's donor list had been in the stolen records. Giving Tuesday was four days away, and her mother was afraid nobody would trust the shelter with a card number again.

"Then we ask them who they are," Priya said, mostly to herself. "And we believe them only when they prove it."

She wrote the rule herself, slowly. Agents that named their human and carried a signed receipt from that human's bank would get through. Agents that would not say would wait in a short, polite line for a person to check them in the morning. Wren tested the rule against the night's traffic and found that the card testing bots, asked to sign, simply left.

At ten past three, the failure rate fell to zero. Priya pressed the button.

Just before dawn her own shopping agent sent a quiet note to her phone. It had noticed the shelter in her messages and offered to schedule a gift for Giving Tuesday, with a matching amount found in her budget.

She looked at it for a long moment.

"Thanks," she typed. "I'll do that one myself."

This week's story was inspired by three real headlines. Gap using Cognition's Devin agent to find and fix website glitches ahead of Black Friday, with human engineers reviewing every fix. Meta and Amazon negotiating over Amazon's blockade of Meta's Muse shopping agent, with advertising and search results as the sticking points. And cyberattacks on DonorPerfect and GoFundMe Pro, the fundraising platforms nonprofits rely on, just ahead of Giving Tuesday, with AI bots suspected.
